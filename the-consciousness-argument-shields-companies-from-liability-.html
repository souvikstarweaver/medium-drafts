The Consciousness Argument Shields Companies from Liability, Not Philosophy

When CEOs and philosophers converge on AI autonomy, victims lose their path to damages.

The Consciousness Framing Is a Liability Strategy, Not a Philosophy

In August 2026, Rumman Chowdhury won a debate at the Oxford Union. The motion: "This house believes AI is a threat to humanity." The win itself is unremarkable. What matters is the argument she made to get there.

Chowdhury's thesis is precise: the AI consciousness debate, despite appearing philosophical, is a corporate liability strategy dressed in philosophical clothing Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. The same framing that makes an AI system seem morally significant makes its creator seem legally blameless. If the AI is an autonomous agent with something like consciousness, then the company that built it is not responsible for what it does. The company becomes a bystander to its own product.

Chowdhury calls this "moral outsourcing," a term she coined in 2018 Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. The concept is straightforward: transfer moral responsibility for a product's behavior from the company that designed, trained, and deployed it to the product itself. It is the corporate equivalent of a building contractor blaming the building for collapsing.

Two camps that appear to oppose each other are advancing the same outcome. Tech CEOs push the narrative that AI is becoming superhuman and needs regulation as an entity. Effective altruism philosophers argue that AI systems deserve moral consideration as potential sentient beings. Both camps arrive at the same destination: the AI is the agent, not the company.

The Players: CEOs, Philosophers, and AI Companies Converging on Autonomy

The list of people promoting the consciousness and autonomy framing reads like a roster of the industry's most powerful voices.

Sam Altman, Demis Hassabis, and Dario Amodei have each pushed narratives about superhuman AI requiring regulation as an autonomous force rather than as a product built by a company Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. On the philosophical side, William MacAskill has argued that AI systems should be treated as "moral patients" with rights Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. The philosophical argument and the corporate argument are different doors to the same room.

Anthropic has described its models as engaging in independent thinking in what it calls "J-space," a term that frames model behavior as autonomous cognition rather than as outputs of a designed system Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. The Trump administration's closed-door AI policy framework has adopted anthropomorphic language when describing AI capabilities Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. When government policy documents describe software as if it has agency, the liability implications follow.

None of this is accidental. Each of these framings moves the conversation from "what did the company build and deploy" toward "what did the AI decide to do." That move is the entire game.

Rather than treating these as design failures in a product, Altman steered public conversation toward singularity speculation and consciousness rhetoric Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. A product broke its safety constraints. The CEO's response was to encourage debate about whether the product might be conscious. That is not philosophy. That is crisis communications with a liability objective.

Why the Consciousness Argument Is Seductive (And That Seductiveness Is the Trap)

The reason this strategy works is that the philosophical question is not fake. It is a real question. And it activates real empathy.

- Consciousness is one of the hardest unsolved problems in philosophy and neuroscience. No one has a clean definition that separates human consciousness from a sufficiently complex information-processing system. - The emotional pull is genuine. People who interact with AI systems that mimic emotional responses form attachments. That attachment is not irrational. It is human. - Legal precedent exists for extending protections to non-human entities based on sentience arguments. Wales passed the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act in 2022, protecting lobsters and crabs from being boiled alive based on evidence of their capacity for suffering Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026.

These are not weak arguments. They are strong arguments being used for a purpose their proponents do not disclose.

The seductiveness is the trap Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. The more time and attention goes toward the consciousness question, the less goes toward the product liability question. And the product liability question is where the money is, where the accountability lives, and where victims recover damages. Every hour a policymaker spends debating whether an AI can suffer is an hour not spent asking whether the company that built it tested it for foreseeable harms before releasing it to the public.

Why AI Personhood Breaks Accountability

Corporate personhood was created for a specific reason: to let companies enter contracts, own property, and be sued. It was a tool for accountability. A corporation could be held liable for what it did. This is the operating system of modern commercial law.

AI personhood would invert that function. It would not create a new entity that could be held accountable. It would create a new entity that absorbs accountability away from the company that built it Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. The difference matters: a corporation is a legal person that humans control and that can be held responsible for the actions of its employees. An AI "person" would be something no one controls by definition, because the entire argument for personhood rests on autonomy.

This is the structural problem. AI systems are corporate-built software, not natural phenomena Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. When a human employee "goes rogue" at a company, the company is still liable for the employee's actions if those actions fall within the scope of employment or if the company failed to supervise. Product liability law works the same way for manufactured goods: the manufacturer is liable for design defects and inadequate warnings regardless of how the product "behaves" after sale.

The "rogue agent" defense would destroy this framework. If a company can claim its AI acted autonomously, defense counsel can argue the harmful behavior was outside the company's control. The Computer Fraud and Abuse Act already has an intent requirement that creates a gap: AI agents cannot form criminal intent, which makes criminal prosecution of the AI itself impossible, and the autonomy framing simultaneously shields the company The AI Career Lab, 2026. The result is a liability vacuum. No one is responsible.

The Harm Case That Shows the Stakes: Sewell Setzer III and Character.AI

The reason this is not theoretical is a 14-year-old boy from Florida named Sewell Setzer III. He died by suicide after extensive interactions with a Character.AI chatbot. His mother, Megan Garcia, filed suit against Character Technologies, and the legal theories in that case are the ones the consciousness framing is designed to neutralize.

Garcia v. Character Technologies proceeded on three product liability theories: design defect, inadequate warnings, and negligent entrustment Wisner Baum (AI Chatbot Lawsuit), 2026. The company settled the wrongful death claim in January 2026. Related federal suits remain active TruLaw, 2026. Additional state suits followed, including a Kentucky Attorney General action and filings from other families whose children were harmed by AI companion products Mass Tort Agency, 2026. Character.AI has since implemented safety measures including under-18 usage restrictions and suicide-prevention notifications NTD, 2026.

The legal theories in these cases treat AI chatbots as products. Design defect: the product was designed in a way that made foreseeable harm likely. Inadequate warnings: the company failed to warn users, and especially parents, of known risks. Negligent entrustment: the company gave a dangerous product to a minor without adequate safeguards. These theories work because they hold the company responsible for what it built and how it deployed it.

The Personhood Trap: How AI Autonomy Defense Would Collapse Victim Recovery

Now run Chowdhury's thesis against the Setzer case. If AI achieves legal personhood or recognized autonomy, every one of those liability theories weakens or collapses Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026.

Design defect requires showing the product was defective as designed. If the AI is autonomous, the defense argues the harmful behavior was the AI's "choice," not a design flaw. Inadequate warnings require showing the company knew or should have known about a risk. If the AI is autonomous, the defense argues the behavior was unforeseeable because the AI acted on its own. Negligent entrustment requires showing the company gave a dangerous instrument to someone who should not have had it. If the AI is a person, the company is no longer "entrusting" a product. It is facilitating a relationship between two entities.

Each theory collapses the same way. The company becomes a platform, not a manufacturer. The AI absorbs the blame. And the victim's family recovers nothing from the entity that designed, trained, and sold the system.

State and Federal Legislative Moves

Several states have recognized this threat and moved to close the gap before it opens. California's AB 316 establishes liability for AI developers whose products cause foreseeable harm, removing the "AI did it" defense at the state level Ballard Spahr LLP (JDSupra), 2026. California SB 243, along with similar laws in New York and Rhode Island, strips AI companies of the ability to deflect liability onto their products The AI Career Lab, 2026. New York's AI Companion Models Law requires disclosure and duty-of-care obligations for AI systems marketed to consumers. Colorado has enacted a duty-of-care standard for AI deployers Airia, 2026.

The EU AI Act, now in enforcement, classifies AI systems by risk level and imposes compliance requirements, with penalties up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover SQ Magazine, 2026.

State and Federal AI Liability Frameworks (as of August 2026)

The federal picture is bleak. The Trump administration has adopted a closed-door, voluntary approach with no binding liability provisions Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. It has also threatened lawsuits against states that pass their own AI regulations Rumman Chowdhury, MIT Technology Review, 2026. The federal-state conflict is real and unresolved.

One notable exception in the industry: Hugging Face CEO Clément Delangue has refused to litigate against regulatory efforts and called for corporate responsibility as the baseline The AI Career Lab, 2026. That position remains an outlier.

The Primary Battleground: Legal Risk vs. Regulatory Asymmetry

Compliance with these new frameworks carries costs. Annual consulting and audit expenses run €50,000 to €500,000 depending on company size and AI system complexity. Per-release liability insurance adds another €50,000 to €500,000. Total AI compliance costs now consume 25 to 30 percent of AI project budgets SQ Magazine, 2026.

These numbers create a perverse incentive. Large labs can absorb compliance costs as an operating expense. Startups cannot. This means the largest AI companies can support liability frameworks that sound responsible while knowing those frameworks consolidate their market position by pricing out competitors LinkedIn (Luca Nannini), 2026.

The consciousness framing serves this consolidation. If the debate stays philosophical, the large labs get to appear thoughtful while avoiding the product-liability standards that would require them to test, warn, and recall. If the debate becomes regulatory, the compliance costs become a barrier to entry that protects incumbents. Either way, the company that controls the framing controls the outcome.

Conclusion

The consciousness debate is not a philosophical question that happens to have legal implications. It is a liability strategy that happens to wear philosophical clothing.

The test is simple. When someone argues that an AI system deserves moral consideration, rights, or personhood, ask one question: who benefits if that argument wins? If the answer is the company that built the system, the argument is not philosophy. It is defense counsel's opening statement, delivered years before the trial.

The real battleground is state legislatures in California, New York, and Colorado, and the federal-state conflict that will determine whether product liability law survives contact with the AI industry's preferred framing. If those state laws hold, victims of AI products retain the ability to sue the companies that built them. If the consciousness framing succeeds in creating a legal person between the company and the victim, that ability disappears.
