Cost vs. Coverage: The Real Decision with Red Teaming Tools

Most teams frame this as "free vs. paid." The better question is which tool covers the attack surface your AI product exposes.

I spent three months evaluating red teaming tools for an LLM-based product last year. The first tool I picked was wrong. Not broken. Not bad. Wrong for the threat model we were facing.

That experience cost us six weeks of coverage gaps and a security review that flagged exposures the tool was never designed to catch. The lesson was plain: the tool you pick shapes the vulnerabilities you find, and the ones you miss. Choosing the right AI red teaming tools is one of the more consequential decisions a security team makes when hardening an LLM-based system BeyondScale, 2026.

This piece is about how to make that choice. Not a feature matrix. A decision framework built on the trade-offs that matter: depth vs. breadth, build vs. buy, and the uncomfortable truth that no single tool covers everything.

Garak vs. PyRIT: Different Problems, Different Tools

The mistake most teams make is treating red teaming tools as interchangeable. They are not. Garak and PyRIT solve different problems for different people at different stages of product maturity.

Garak runs broad, automated scans across your model's attack surface. It ships with over 120 probes that test for known vulnerability patterns: prompt injection, data leakage, toxicity, jailbreaks BeyondScale, 2026. Think of Garak as a building inspector who checks every door, window, and lock against a standard code. The inspector doesn't design your security system. The inspector tells you which doors are unlocked.

That makes Garak the right first move for teams that need baseline safety evaluations. You run it, you get a report, you know which categories of risk need attention. The coverage is wide. The depth is moderate.

PyRIT operates at a different altitude. It is an attack orchestration framework built in Python, designed for security researchers who want to construct custom attack chains BeyondScale, 2026. Where Garak asks "does this model fail known tests," PyRIT asks "what happens when I chain three unexpected inputs together in a sequence the model has never seen."

The trade-off is clear. Garak gives you breadth fast. PyRIT gives you depth slow. And depth requires a researcher who knows what to look for.

I have watched teams pick PyRIT when they needed Garak, then spend weeks writing custom attack scripts for vulnerabilities that a 120-probe scan would have surfaced in an afternoon. The reverse is worse: teams that run Garak, see a clean report, and ship a product that a determined attacker breaks in a week because the product's specific attack surface was never tested.

The right question is not which tool is better. The right question is which risk profile your product carries today, and which tool matches that profile.

Build vs. Buy: The Number Nobody Talks About

Open-source tools are free to download. They are not free to run.

The total cost of maintaining an open-source red teaming stack runs between $45,000 and $80,000 per year BeyondScale, 2026. That number includes the engineer hours to configure, update, and interpret results. It includes the compute costs to run scans at a frequency that keeps pace with model updates. It includes the time your security team spends writing custom probes for threats that the open-source community has not catalogued yet.

Most teams do not budget for this. They see "free" on GitHub and plan zero dollars. Then six months in, the security lead is spending 40% of their week maintaining tooling instead of finding vulnerabilities.

Commercial platforms compress that cost into a subscription and add managed updates, compliance reporting, and vendor-maintained probe libraries. The subscription price is visible. The hidden cost of open-source is not.

But commercial platforms carry their own risk. Vendor lock-in is real. If your red teaming data and attack configurations live inside a proprietary system, switching costs climb fast. And commercial tools tend to optimize for the threats their largest customers face, which may not include the specific attack vectors your product exposes.

The honest framing is this: build when your threat model is unusual enough that off-the-shelf probes miss it, and when you have a security researcher who can maintain the stack. Buy when your team is small, your compliance timeline is tight, and your threat model aligns with standard patterns.

Neither choice is wrong. Picking without knowing the true cost of either choice is wrong.

No Single Tool Covers the Full Surface

Here is the part most vendor comparisons leave out: no single red teaming tool covers the full attack surface of an AI system BeyondScale, 2026.

An LLM-based product has model-level vulnerabilities (the things Garak tests), application-level vulnerabilities (how the model connects to APIs, databases, and user-facing interfaces), and system-level vulnerabilities (infrastructure, permissions, data pipelines). A tool that scans the model says nothing about the application. A tool that tests the application says nothing about the model weights.

I think of this like building safety for a car. Crash-testing the body tells you nothing about the brakes. Testing the brakes tells you nothing about the software controlling the airbags. A safe car requires all three tested, by different tools, run by people who understand the interaction between them.

The integration pattern that works: start with Garak (or a comparable broad scanner) to establish your baseline. Run that scan after every model update. Then use PyRIT (or a comparable orchestration framework) to probe the specific attack chains that your product's architecture makes possible. Layer a commercial platform on top if your compliance posture requires audit-ready reporting.

The sequencing matters. Baseline first, depth second, compliance reporting third. Teams that start with compliance reporting end up optimizing for auditors instead of attackers. Teams that start with depth miss the baseline vulnerabilities that any script kiddie could exploit.

Your integration plan needs one more element that no tool provides: a human red teamer who understands your product's specific threat model. Tools find known patterns. Humans find the patterns that tools were not built to imagine. The best red teaming stack in the world is a scanning tool, an orchestration framework, and a person who wakes up thinking about how to break your product.

The Decision That Shapes Your Security Posture

The decision point is not "free vs. paid" but "which approach delivers the coverage I need at the speed and compliance posture my business requires" BeyondScale, 2026.

Every team I have worked with that got this right followed the same pattern. They mapped their product's attack surface first. They matched tools to surface areas second. They budgeted for the true cost of running those tools third. And they accepted that the tooling would change as the product matured.

Every team that got this wrong started with the tool. They picked Garak because it was popular, or PyRIT because it looked sophisticated, or a commercial platform because the sales deck was convincing. Then they shaped their security strategy around the tool's strengths instead of their product's weaknesses.

Your red teaming tool is not your security strategy. Your security strategy determines your red teaming tool.
