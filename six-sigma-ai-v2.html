The Worst Place to Use Six Sigma Is Your AI Roadmap

And the best place is three layers below it.

The Company That Measured Itself Out of New Ideas

In 2001, a slow-growing 3M hired James McNerney from General Electric to fix its margins. He brought GE's favourite tool with him: Six Sigma.

Within a few years, more than 55,000 employees had completed Six Sigma training. Costs fell. The stock rose.

Over the same years, the share of 3M's sales from new products slid from about a third to a quarter (Design News, 2007). 3M has since called that metric a poor gauge and stopped publishing it.

When McNerney left for Boeing in 2005, his successor George Buckley kept Six Sigma in the factories and the supply chain and pulled it back from the research labs.

Eric Ries tells this story in Incorruptible, his book on why good companies drift from what made them good. Most readers take it as a warning about innovation.

AI teams should take it as a warning about their roadmap.

The pressures that sent Six Sigma into 3M's labs are building inside AI companies today. Inference bills keep growing. Boards want predictable margins.

Discipline sounds like the answer. In the right layer, it is.

Six Sigma Fixes What Already Works

Six Sigma is easiest to understand on a factory floor.

A soda plant fills bottles to 500 ml, with 5 ml of tolerance either way. About 2% come out underfilled. A Six Sigma team works through five steps, known as DMAIC:

  • Define. The team scopes the problem to one line, one machine and one number: fill volume.
  • Measure. The team checks that the scales agree with each other, then samples bottles for two weeks to set a baseline.
  • Analyze. The data points to two causes: worn seals on two nozzles, and soda that foams when it runs warm.
  • Improve. New seals and a chiller go in. A pilot run confirms the fix against the baseline.
  • Control. A control chart goes on the line, so any drift triggers action within the hour.

Underfills drop from 2% to near zero. Nobody invented anything. The team made an existing process stop wobbling.

That is the whole design. DMAIC fixes processes that already exist, hold still, and can be measured. Its enemy is variation.

A sister method, Design for Six Sigma, handles new products. It comes back later in this piece.

The Research Saw This Coming

One company is an anecdote. Two decades of patent data make it a pattern.

Mary Benner and Michael Tushman followed paint and photography companies from 1980 to 1999. As process management programs spread inside those firms, their innovation tilted toward building on what they already knew, and exploratory innovation lost ground (Benner and Tushman, 2002).

Their follow-up work argued that these programs belong in stable operations, with a wall between them and exploratory work.

A study of 83 Six Sigma projects inside a large automaker found the same split. Six Sigma improved incremental innovation and financial results. The data showed no evidence that it helped radical innovation (Oprime et al., 2019).

Six Sigma does not kill innovation. It kills one kind of innovation.

The kind that starts without an answer.

AI Work Starts Without an Answer

When Microsoft studied its own teams building AI features, it found that the workflow loops back on itself again and again, and that models tangle together in ways that make errors hard to predict (Amershi et al., 2019).

In plain terms, a change in one place makes something you never touched better or worse.

A bottling line holds still while you measure it. An AI product does not.

The clearest test is one feature run through DMAIC: a support assistant that answers customer questions about refunds. A defect is a wrong or invented answer.

DMAIC Meets a Support Assistant

Define

The bottling line has a spec: 500 ml, plus or minus 5.

A refund answer has no spec. It can be accurate and confusing. Correct and cold.

It can be true to the policy and wrong for the customer. Two experts can read the same reply and disagree on whether it counts as a defect.

Measure

Here the method breaks.

A scale weighs a bottle. For thousands of refund answers, teams use an LLM judge, a second AI model that grades the first one.

The judge is an AI too.

Two runs on the same answer can return two different scores. Judges favour longer answers, the option they read first, and writing that sounds like their own. A vendor update to the judge model can move your baseline overnight while your product stays untouched.

So teams add human reviewers to check the judge. This is human-in-the-loop review.

The humans disagree with each other. If two reviewers agree on 80% of answers, your ground truth carries 20% noise. No one can measure a defect rate in parts per million with an instrument that argues with itself one time in five.

The problems compound.

Reviewer corrections feed back into prompts and training data, so the act of measuring changes the process in the middle of the baseline.

Reviewers catch some errors before customers see them, so the defect rate you record blends two processes: how often the model fails and how often the reviewer misses.

Reviewers tire. Rubrics drift over weeks. Human review costs money, so samples stay small.

On the bottling line, Measure takes two weeks. On an AI product, Measure turns into its own research project. The team has to fix the measuring system before it can fix the product.

Analyze

The bottling team found two causes and proved both with data.

An AI failure often has no single cause. The behaviour comes from training data, prompt wording and model version all at once. A fishbone diagram has no box for "the model learned an odd pattern somewhere in its training."

Improve

A new seal stays a new seal.

A prompt change that fixes refund answers can break shipping answers two screens away. Every fix needs a full regression run, and the next model release can undo all of it.

Control

Six Sigma ends by locking the process in place.

Locking an AI product means turning down better models when they arrive. The discipline that protects a bottling line becomes the reason an AI product falls behind.

Under all five steps sits one assumption: variation is waste.

For a generative product, variation is part of the value. Answers adapt to the person asking. A team that squeezes out the variance squeezes out the value with it.

Design for Six Sigma Hits the Same Wall

A Six Sigma practitioner has a fair reply here. DMAIC fixes existing processes. New products get Design for Six Sigma, run as DMADV: Define, Measure, Analyze, Design, Verify.

The reply does not survive contact with AI.

DFSS opens by capturing what customers want and turning it into fixed, measurable requirements. That works when customers can describe what they want.

AI customers can't. Before chat models, no requirements document said "let me talk to my spreadsheet." The need appeared after the capability did.

AI requirements get discovered, not captured. A method that needs the spec upfront stays one model release behind.

Three Layers Where Six Sigma Wins

The roadmap is the wrong place for Six Sigma. Three layers underneath it are the right place, because they behave like a bottling line: stable, measurable, and expensive when they wobble.

The data layer. Missing fields, schema breaks and duplicate records are defects in the factory sense. Annotation is a human process with measurable agreement rates. Six Sigma was built for this.

The serving layer. Latency, uptime and cost per query have clear specs and traceable causes. When response times creep up, a root-cause analysis finds the culprit.

The release layer. Once a team trusts an eval, holding every release to it is quality control. A control chart on output quality catches slow decay before customers do.

3M's researchers kept design of experiments, one of Six Sigma's core tools, after the mandate ended (Design News, 2007).

The tool was never the problem. The blanket mandate was.

The Lab and the Line

Buckley's fix at 3M works for AI teams. The work splits into two zones.

The Lab is where the team learns what the model can do. Prompt experiments, new capabilities, model evaluations and product bets live here. Most experiments fail, and the failures teach.

The Lab gets judged on what it learns, not on its defect rate.

The Line is where proven capabilities run at scale. The three layers live here, and Six Sigma earns its place: service targets, control charts, regression gates and root-cause analysis.

The handoff matters most.

A capability graduates from the Lab to the Line when two conditions hold. Its spec has stopped changing. The team trusts the eval that measures it.

Before graduation, Six Sigma slows the team down. After graduation, skipping it produces a product nobody can rely on.

Benner and Tushman called this ambidexterity: exploration and exploitation inside one company, kept far enough apart that one cannot smother the other.

What 3M Got, and What You Will Get

McNerney's mistake was not bringing Six Sigma to 3M. It was bringing it everywhere.

Ries traces how the pressure of success bends companies away from their purpose. AI companies feel that pressure now. Inference bills, investor timelines and margin targets all point toward more discipline, everywhere.

Some of that discipline will help.

Every AI team that runs its Lab like a Line will get what 3M got.

Excellent margins. Nothing new to sell.
