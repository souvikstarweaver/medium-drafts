Every Frontier Model Asks More Questions Than Optimal and Stops Too Late

Without a cost-aware stopping rule, models keep gathering context past the point it pays for itself.

The Finding: Frontier Models Ask More Questions Than Optimal

Every frontier model on the market today asks too many questions before acting. Not some of them. All of them.

A recent paper tested seven models, including GPT-5, GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Gemini 2.0 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Haiku 4.5, and Grok 4, on a structured information-gathering task based on the "20 Questions" game format. Each model had to identify a target by asking yes-or-no questions, and each question carried a cost. Across all difficulty tiers, every model asked more questions than a mathematically optimal agent would need arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026.

The optimal agent uses a stopping rule that works like a thermostat for information: keep gathering context only when the expected knowledge gained from the next question exceeds the cost of asking it. The formal version says that the expected information gain about the answer, given a potential question, must be greater than a cost penalty scaled by a parameter λ arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026. When the information you would gain drops below the price you would pay, stop asking and act.

Think of it like a mechanic running diagnostics on a car. Each test costs time and money. A good mechanic stops testing once the remaining tests would cost more than the value of the information they would reveal. These models are the mechanic who runs every test in the book, even after the problem is obvious.

The Oracle and the Rule

The paper defines optimality through dynamic programming, a method that works backward from the goal to compute the cheapest possible path to the right answer. The oracle considers every possible question, calculates how each one would split the remaining candidates into "yes" and "no" groups weighted by their likelihood, and picks the question that minimizes total expected cost arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026. The stopping rule falls out of this computation: when no question's expected information gain exceeds λ times its cost, the optimal move is to commit to an answer. Under deterministic observations (where you get a clean yes or no, not a fuzzy response), information gain equals entropy reduction, the shrinking of uncertainty about the target arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026.

What the Framework Separates: Belief, Measurement, and Preference

The deeper contribution is not the observation that models over-ask. It is the framework that explains why and gives builders a tool to fix it.

Active inference, which originated in neuroscience as a model of how brains make decisions, works by minimizing expected free energy, a single quantity that combines two things: how bad an outcome would be (risk) and how much you would learn by acting (epistemic value) arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026. The formula G_t(a) computes the expected "badness" of an action minus the expected "learning" from that action. When an agent has high uncertainty and learning is cheap, the math favors asking. When uncertainty is low or asking is expensive, the math favors acting.

What makes this useful for practitioners is that the framework separates three things that agentic systems today mash together arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026.

Three Roles That Matter

Belief state is the agent's current model of the world, its probability distribution over possible answers. In most production agents, this is implicit in the context window. It is never inspected, never quantified, and never tracked across turns.

Measurement model defines what a question or tool call will tell you and what it will cost. In production, this is absent. A model issues a function call with no estimate of how much it will learn or what the call costs in latency, tokens, or dollars.

Preference model defines what a good outcome looks like: the user's intent, the loss function, the business constraint. In production, this lives in the system prompt as natural language, disconnected from the decision loop.

Conflating these three roles is a design smell. When the belief state, the measurement model, and the preference model are tangled together in a single prompt, the agent has no mechanism to reason about whether the next action is worth its cost. It defaults to asking, because asking is the safe choice when you cannot compute the trade-off.

The 9x Improvement from One Targeted Clarification

The stopping rule is not a theoretical nicety. It produces measurable gains in a practical task.

The paper tested a prompt autocompletion scenario: an agent receives a partial product-description prompt and must decide whether to ask the user a clarifying question before generating the output. The baseline approach generates the product description with no clarification. The active-inference approach uses the stopping rule to decide whether one clarification question is worth the token cost arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026.

The results: the active weighted policy achieved 0.375 compliance with the user's desired attributes (format, length, tone) at approximately 219 tokens, compared to 0.0417 compliance at approximately 112 tokens for the baseline. That is a nine-times improvement in output quality for roughly double the token spend arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026.

The mechanism is precise. Each clarifiable attribute carries a relevance weight: format gets 1.0, length gets 0.6, tone gets 0.25. Clarification costs scale with specificity: 24 tokens for a short question, 48 for a medium one, 72 for a detailed one. A deterministic verifier checks whether the generated output matches the clarified attribute arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026. The agent does not ask about everything. It asks about the attribute where the information gain, weighted by relevance, exceeds the token cost of the question. One targeted clarification, chosen by the rule, does more than scattering questions across all attributes.

This is the difference between a support agent that asks five clarifying questions before helping you and one that asks the single question that matters.

Cost-Aware Prompt Variant Selection: The ARC Experiment

The stopping rule works beyond question-answering dialogues. The paper tested it on a different problem: automated prompt selection for a reasoning benchmark.

The setup uses ARC-Challenge, a multiple-choice science reasoning test, with six prompt variants: letter_only (pick the answer letter), short_reasoning (explain briefly), eliminate_two (remove wrong answers first), keyword_match (find key terms), units_and_scales (check dimensional consistency), and contrastive_explanations (compare answer choices). Each variant costs a different number of tokens. The agent must decide which prompt variant to try next, given a fixed budget of 120,000 tokens arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026.

The mutual information (MI) policy selects the next prompt variant by computing how much uncertainty about the correct answer that variant would reduce, divided by its token cost. The formula takes the current entropy (the agent's remaining uncertainty), subtracts the expected entropy after seeing the result, and divides by the cost of running that variant arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026. The expected free energy (EFE) policy adds a twist: an epistemic weight that decays over time, so the agent shifts from exploring new prompt variants early to exploiting the best-known variant later.

The information efficiency metric measures this precisely: how much entropy the agent reduces per 1,000 tokens spent. The formula is 1000 times the change in entropy divided by the tokens consumed arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026.

The principle is the same as the clarification experiment, wearing different clothes. Every action the agent can take, whether asking a question, choosing a prompt, or calling a tool, has an information yield and a cost. The stopping rule provides the math to compare them.

Where the Paper Stops and Production Begins

The framework is clean. Production is not.

The paper tests on structured tasks where every question gets a deterministic yes-or-no answer arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026. Real users give vague, contradictory, or incomplete responses. A user who says "make it more professional" has not given you a deterministic observation. The entropy-reduction math assumes you can cleanly update your belief after each answer. Noisy observations break that assumption.

Tools are absent from all experiments. The paper notes this as a deliberate scope restriction: tool calls were disabled to isolate the information-gathering behavior arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026. In production, agents call APIs, query databases, and read documents. Each tool call has latency, error rates, and rate limits that do not appear in the cost function.

The prompt autocompletion experiment uses simulated user answers, not real human responses arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026. Simulated users are predictable. Real users change their minds, misunderstand the question, and introduce information the agent did not model.

State explosion is the scaling wall. The dynamic programming oracle becomes computationally intractable for large candidate sets. The paper acknowledges that greedy approximations, sampling, or learned lookahead would be needed for real-world scale arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026.

These are not reasons to dismiss the framework. They are the engineering problems that sit between the principle and the deployment.

What to Build Next: Three Design Principles

Model cost in the decision loop. Every action your agent can take, a function call, a clarifying question, a retrieval query, has a measurable cost in tokens, latency, and dollars. Right now, most agents treat all actions as free. They are not. Add a cost field to every action in your agent's action space. Without it, the stopping rule has nothing to operate on.

Separate belief, measurement, and preference. If your agent's belief state (what it knows), its measurement model (what it expects to learn from each action), and its preference model (what counts as success) are all encoded in a single system prompt, cost signals have nowhere to flow. Track the agent's uncertainty over possible answers as an explicit distribution, even a rough one. Compute expected information gain before issuing each action. Keep the preference model distinct so you can tune the cost threshold λ without rewriting the prompt.

Test against a simple oracle. Greedy information gain, picking the single question with the highest gain right now, is myopic. It only looks one step ahead arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026. Even one additional step of lookahead changes the optimal question sequence. Build a baseline oracle for your domain, even a toy one, and compare your agent's question count against it. The gap between your agent's behavior and the oracle's behavior is the cost of not having a stopping rule.

Conclusion

The finding is not that frontier models are bad at gathering information. They reduce uncertainty with every turn. The finding is that they lack a cost model, so they keep reducing uncertainty past the point where the next question is worth less than the next action arXiv:2608.19202, "Active Inference as Context Acquisition for AI Agents," 2026.

That is not a model deficiency. It is a missing design choice. The stopping rule exists. The math is tractable for structured domains. The gap between the paper and production is engineering work, not theoretical work. The next agent you build should know the price of its own curiosity.
