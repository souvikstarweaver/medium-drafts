The Watermark Cannot Tell You Who Wrote It

Anthropic's global deployment signals Claude processed the text, not that Claude authored it

Why Anthropic Deployed Watermarking, and Did It Globally

On August 11, 2026, Anthropic turned on text watermarking across every Claude access channel. Not a beta. Not a regional rollout to satisfy a single regulator. A global deployment covering the API, consumer products, and third-party integrations TechCrunch, 2026.

The EU AI Act requires provenance marking for AI-generated content. Anthropic could have scoped the feature to EU users and called it compliance. Instead, the company applied the watermark to all text outputs, everywhere, for every user TechTimes, 2026. That scope decision matters. It tells you Anthropic sees watermarking not as a regulatory checkbox but as a default property of Claude's output Mashable, 2026.

The deployment is the most ambitious production-scale text watermarking any frontier lab has attempted. And the gap between what it accomplishes and what people will assume it accomplishes is where the real story lives.

How the Watermark Works: Tournament Sampling at the Token Level

The mechanism is less exotic than it sounds. Sebastian Raschka put it plainly: the watermarking is nothing more than fixing a random seed and making the sampling process deterministic at specific decision points Sebastian Raschka, 2026.

Here is the sequence, step by step:

- A secret key, derived from the watermarking context and the four tokens preceding each decision point, sets a random seed. That seed determines how the model chooses its next word at moments where multiple options are close in probability. - The watermark is applied only where multiple tokens are near-equally plausible. When the model is certain about the next word (say, completing "San Fran" with "cisco"), no watermark is inserted. The mark lives in the gray zones, the places where the model could go several ways. - At those gray-zone moments, candidate tokens are assigned bit signatures and compared in tournament brackets. The bracket structure biases the selection toward a pattern that is invisible to a human reader but recoverable by anyone who holds the secret key Roman Eng (LinkedIn), 2026. - Detection is computationally cheap. Unlike older watermarking approaches, verification does not require rerunning the full model on the suspect text. The key holder checks whether the statistical pattern of token choices matches what the seeded tournament would produce. - The technique descends from Google DeepMind's SynthID-Text and the green-list bias research by Kirchenbauer et al. at ICML 2023 Sebastian Raschka, 2026.

Think of it like a casino where the house has loaded certain dice. The dice still land on valid numbers. A player watching the game sees normal rolls. But someone who knows which dice are loaded and at which tables can reconstruct the house's fingerprint from a long enough sequence of outcomes.

| Stage of Sampling | Standard LLM Process | With Watermark Applied | |---|---|---| | Token candidates ranked | Model scores all possible next tokens by probability | Same ranking, no change | | Selection at high-certainty points | Top token chosen (near-deterministic) | Top token chosen, no watermark inserted | | Selection at low-certainty points | Random sampling weighted by probability distribution | Secret-key seed fixes the random selection via tournament brackets | | Output to user | Text appears natural | Text appears identical but carries a statistical fingerprint |

What the Watermark Tells You, and What It Doesn't

Here is the distinction most coverage has missed. The watermark signals that Claude processed the text. It does not signal that Claude authored the text TechTimes, 2026.

If you paste your own essay into Claude and ask it to fix three commas, the output carries a watermark. If you ask Claude to rewrite one paragraph of a ten-page document, the entire response carries a watermark. The mark does not distinguish between "Claude generated this from scratch" and "Claude touched this for five seconds."

That conflation is not a bug in the implementation. It is a structural property of how the watermark is applied: at the token-sampling layer, on every output, regardless of how much of the text originated with the user.

For anyone building a detection policy on top of this watermark, the difference between "processed by Claude" and "written by Claude" is the difference between a useful provenance signal and a false accusation.

The Removal Problem, and Why It Favors Bad Actors

The watermark is embedded in statistical patterns across token sequences. Any operation that rewrites enough of those tokens destroys the pattern.

Paraphrasing the output manually works. Running the text through a second language model works. Translating to another language and back works. Even aggressive editing by a human writer can break the token sequence enough to erase the signal Sebastian Raschka, 2026.

Multiple guides for removing Claude watermarks appeared within days of the deployment WriteBros.ai, 2026 HaiMaker.ai, 2026. The techniques are not sophisticated. Run the output through a local open-source model. Ask it to paraphrase. Done.

Anthropologic has not published the threshold for how much text must survive intact for the watermark to remain detectable The Creators AI, 2026. Without that threshold, no one outside Anthropic can assess how fragile or robust the mark is against partial edits.

The asymmetry here is stark. A careful writer who uses Claude to improve their own prose carries an indelible (to them) watermark they cannot see or verify. A bad actor generating thousands of words of AI content has the technical knowledge and motivation to strip the mark in seconds using a free local model. The watermark burdens the cautious user and fails to constrain the adversarial one.

The Detection Gap: What the Absence of a Mark Means

Two failure modes sit on opposite sides of the same problem.

False positives: independent testing has shown that text with common n-gram patterns (repeated phrases, formulaic structures) can trigger AI-detection tools even when written by a human. Andrea Saez tested Claude's watermark detector and found cases where the detector flagged human-written text that happened to share structural patterns with Claude output Andrea Saez, 2026. Others have reported the King James Bible triggering certain AI detectors, not because scripture was written by a language model, but because its repetitive phrasing mimics the statistical patterns detectors look for.

False negatives: any text that has been paraphrased, rewritten, or run through a second model produces zero watermark signal. The detection API returns a clean result. The text is still AI-generated. The mark is gone.

The dangerous assumption is this: the absence of a watermark means a human wrote the text. It does not. It means either a human wrote it, or someone stripped the mark. The watermark cannot distinguish between these two cases TechTimes, 2026.

The Predicted Consequence: A Two-Stage Pipeline That Produces Worse Text

Raschka's prediction is specific and worth taking seriously: the most likely market response to production-scale watermarking is a two-stage generation pipeline. Step one, generate the text with Claude (the best model available for the task). Step two, run the output through a cheaper, local, unwatermarked model whose only job is to paraphrase enough tokens to destroy the statistical fingerprint Sebastian Raschka, 2026.

The second model does not know which tokens carry the watermark. It cannot target the mark. It makes arbitrary word substitutions across the entire output, changing words that carried no watermark signal alongside words that did. The result is text that has been degraded by random edits in pursuit of a statistical goal the editing model cannot see.

User Input ↓ Claude (high-capability model) → Watermarked Output ↓ Local Model (cheap, unwatermarked) → Arbitrary Edits ↓ Degraded AI Text (watermark stripped, quality reduced)

The output of this pipeline is worse than what Claude produced and harder to detect than the original watermarked version. The watermark created an incentive to degrade quality.

Why This Gap Matters to You

If you are a writer who uses Claude to polish your own work, you now carry an invisible provenance mark on every output. You cannot see it. You cannot verify it. Removing it requires running your text through another model, which will change words you chose on purpose.

If you run a publication or a hiring process that evaluates written work, the watermark gives you a false binary. Marked text means "Claude touched it," which includes light editing of human work. Unmarked text means either "a human wrote this" or "someone knew enough to strip the mark." The watermark cannot tell you which.

The people who will strip watermarks are the people producing high-volume AI content at scale. The people who will not strip watermarks are the people using Claude as a writing tool in good faith. The enforcement mechanism selects against the users it was designed to protect.

Conclusion

If institutions treat watermark presence as proof of AI authorship, they will punish human writers who used Claude for a comma fix. If they treat watermark absence as proof of human authorship, they will systematically miss stripped AI text. If publishers adopt the two-stage pipeline Raschka predicts, the net effect on the internet's text quality will be negative: more AI-generated content, produced at lower quality, with no detectable provenance.

The watermark is a real technical accomplishment in provenance signaling. It is not an enforcement mechanism. The gap between those two things is where every downstream decision will go wrong.
