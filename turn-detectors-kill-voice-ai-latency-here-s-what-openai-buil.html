Turn Detectors Kill Voice AI Latency. Here's What OpenAI Built Instead

Removing the bottleneck required rethinking transport, state, context, and observability in parallel.

Every voice AI product you've shipped or used has carried the same architectural assumption: someone has to decide when the user stopped talking before the system can respond. That component is the turn detector, and it fails in exactly two directions. Guess too soon, and the user gets cut off mid-sentence. Guess too late, and the response feels sluggish. OpenAI, 2026 described this as a binary failure mode, and the description matches what anyone who has built on top of a cascaded voice pipeline already knows from production.

OpenAI spent six months rearchitecting around this problem. What they built, GPT-Live, required rewriting the transport layer, rethinking state management for unbounded sessions, building new observability infrastructure, and inventing a protocol that starts a voice session with a single UDP packet. The turn detector is gone from the audio path. Everything downstream had to change because of that one decision.

The Turn Detector Trap: Why Previous Voice AI Felt Sluggish

The traditional voice AI pipeline works like a relay race with too many handoffs. User speech goes to a speech-to-text model, the text goes to an LLM, and the LLM's response goes to a text-to-speech model. Each handoff adds latency. Each handoff loses information. Tone, pacing, hesitation, emphasis: all stripped out at the first conversion to text. OpenAI, 2026

Speech-to-speech models eliminated the text middle step, but they kept the turn detector. The model still waited for a binary "user is done" signal before it started generating a response. OpenAI, 2026 The bottleneck moved but never disappeared.

The result was a conversation that felt like talking through a walkie-talkie. Press to talk. Release. Wait. Hear the reply. No interruptions, no backchannels, no "mhmm" while the other side is still explaining. That is not how humans communicate, and users could feel the gap even if they couldn't name it.

Full-Duplex Listening: The Core Decision That Changed Everything

OpenAI's GPT-Live voice model listens and speaks at the same time. It is full-duplex, meaning the audio flows in both directions continuously, the way a phone call works but a walkie-talkie does not. The turn detector is not moved to a different place in the pipeline. It is removed from the audio path entirely. OpenAI, 2026

This one decision created every other engineering problem described in this article.

When the voice model needs to reason about something complex, it delegates to GPT-5.5 on a separate asynchronous path. That delegation happens without interrupting the live audio loop. OpenAI, 2026 The voice model keeps listening and can acknowledge, respond to a new question, or buy time while the reasoning model works. Think of it as a customer service agent who keeps talking to you while a specialist in the back office looks up your account details.

Moving Audio Onto a Dedicated Fast Path

Removing the turn detector only works if nothing else can stall the audio stream. A slow database query, a tool call that takes three seconds, a business-logic check that hits an external API: any of these will freeze the conversation if audio and logic share the same execution path.

OpenAI separated media flow from application logic as a hard architectural boundary. Audio moves on a dedicated fast path. Delegation, tool use, and business logic sit behind an asynchronous RPC boundary, meaning they run in a separate process that communicates through message-passing rather than blocking the audio thread. OpenAI, 2026

The guarantee is not a best practice. It is structural: a slow tool call or backend service can delay its own result, but it cannot stall the flow of media. OpenAI, 2026

To make the fast path fast enough, OpenAI rewrote the media frontend and inference logic in Go, replacing Python asyncio. The result: p95 frame-delivery latency (the worst-case tail latency, where 95% of frames arrive faster) now matches the old system's p50, where only half of frames arrived that quickly. OpenAI, 2026 The floor of the new system is the ceiling of the old one.

Collapsing the Network Handshake: WARP and Instant Connect

A standard WebRTC connection requires six network round trips before the first audio frame moves. For a voice product where the user expects to start talking and hear a response within a fraction of a second, those six round trips are a startup penalty that users feel as dead air.

OpenAI built WARP, a bundle of four backward-compatible improvements to WebRTC: SPED, DTLS 1.3, SNAP, and pre-negotiated data channels. Together, these compress the six round trips into one. OpenAI, 2026 WARP is not a proprietary fork. It is advancing through the IETF TSVWG standards process and has been added to libwebrtc and Pion, meaning other teams can adopt the same improvements. OpenAI, 2026

On top of WARP, OpenAI built Instant Connect. Standard session negotiation (called SDP, the Session Description Protocol exchange) happens when the user clicks "start." Instant Connect pre-negotiates those SDP parameters ahead of time, without reserving server capacity or requiring changes to existing WebRTC implementations. When the pre-negotiated parameters are valid, the server materializes the session when the first media packet arrives. OpenAI, 2026

With WARP and Instant Connect combined, a client can start a voice session with a single UDP packet. OpenAI, 2026 That is as close to zero-latency startup as a networked system allows.

Keeping State Alive: The Compaction Problem

A full-duplex conversation has no natural stopping point. The user can keep talking for ten minutes or two hours. As the conversation grows, the accumulated context (the model's memory of everything said so far) can exceed the model's context limit, the maximum amount of conversation history it can hold. OpenAI, 2026

The standard fix is context compaction: summarize older parts of the conversation and discard the raw history. The problem is that compaction invalidates the model's KV cache (the pre-computed state that lets the model generate responses without re-reading the entire conversation from scratch), which forces a new prefill and introduces delay. OpenAI, 2026

OpenAI treats compaction as a managed transition. While the original model instance keeps chatting, the system compacts context and prepares a replacement instance in the background. Once that instance is ready, the system switches over without media interruption. OpenAI, 2026 The user never knows it happened. This is the same pattern as a blue-green deployment, but applied to a single conversation session instead of a fleet of servers.

Asynchronous Delegation: When the Voice Model Needs Help

When GPT-Live delegates to GPT-5.5, latency depends on how fast the reasoning model can start producing output. OpenAI pre-warms the delegation path: a frontier model inference session is created and prefilled with the conversation's initial context at the start of each voice session, before the first delegation request arrives. OpenAI, 2026

Stable session affinity (keeping requests routed to the same server) and prompt caching (reusing computed context across requests) reduce delegation latency further. Reasoning effort, output limits, tool schemas, and model-tool round trips are all tuned to minimize the time the voice model spends waiting for a reasoning answer. OpenAI, 2026

The voice model fills the gap. It can say "let me check on that," produce a brief acknowledgment, or continue on a different thread while delegation completes. The user experiences a conversation, not a loading spinner.

The Turn-Derivation Cost: Re-imposing Structure on Continuous Speech

Here is the part nobody warns you about when you remove turns from the model path: everything around the model still expects turns.

The ChatGPT UI needs to display messages. Analytics needs to count exchanges. Safety infrastructure needs to evaluate individual utterances. All of these systems operate on the assumption that a conversation is a sequence of discrete user and assistant messages. OpenAI, 2026

OpenAI had to build a layer that derives turn structure from continuous audio. The system maintains two views: a speculative view for the UI, which is real-time and provisional, and an authoritative finalized transcript for logging and analytics. OpenAI, 2026

The judgment calls are subtle. A brief acknowledgment ("mm hmm," "okay") from the assistant while the user is talking is not logged as its own message. A substantive interjection often is. The system must make this distinction in real time, and the distinction has downstream consequences for safety review, billing, and conversation history. OpenAI, 2026

This problem also breaks existing measurement frameworks. Turn-based metrics (time-to-first-token, response latency) assume the model starts responding after the user finishes. In a full-duplex system, the model is always producing output. Roark identified four new metrics that full-duplex systems require: barge-in stop latency (how fast the model stops talking when interrupted), false barge-in rate (how often the model stops when it shouldn't have), backchannel yield rate (how often an "mhmm" mid-turn causes the model to abandon its response), and turn-onset latency (time from user end-of-utterance to first agent audio). Shadow Testing Exposed the Capacity Question You Cannot Avoid

Before launching GPT-Live to production, OpenAI ran shadow testing: routing real traffic patterns through the new system while the old system still served users. The results rewrote their understanding of what "capacity" means for a continuous voice system.

GPU throughput is the standard capacity metric for AI inference. Voice breaks that framing. Voice sessions stay open and stream frames continuously, so CPU-side stream handlers, queues, and network paths must scale alongside inference. OpenAI, 2026

Under real load during shadow testing, a supporting (non-GPU) component saturated earlier than load-test estimates predicted. Inference requests accumulated. Latency compounded. OpenAI, 2026 The capacity question shifted from "How many requests can a GPU handle?" to "How many concurrent sessions can the system sustain while keeping every frame on schedule?"

Shadow testing also made geography a first-order concern. Routing to distant capacity adds delay at startup and during streaming. OpenAI began validating model rollouts together with regional capacity and traffic-steering configuration, and breaking down latency by source geography. OpenAI, 2026

Long Sessions Expose the Bugs That Load Tests Miss

Short load tests simulate bursts. They do not simulate time.

Long-running sessions exposed memory and persistence pressure that never appeared during benchmarks. Reconnects exercised the compaction and state restoration paths in ways synthetic tests never did. Ordinary client disconnects revealed races in the shutdown handshake, where the client and server disagreed about whether the session was ending. OpenAI, 2026

These problems depend on three things that short load tests cannot produce: elapsed time, accumulated state, and behavior across service boundaries. OpenAI, 2026 If you test a voice system with five-minute synthetic calls, you will never see the bugs that appear at minute forty-five.

The Observability Rewrite: From Conflated Metrics to Isolated Paths

Production testing revealed that OpenAI's existing observability was not built for continuous voice. Metrics conflated different latency sources, making it impossible to distinguish media-path delay from delegation-path delay. Dashboards aggregated health across engines, hiding individual unhealthy nodes. Configuration drift between tested and deployed systems went undetected. OpenAI, 2026

The response: more granular telemetry that isolates each path, validation against known-good configurations before rollout, staged ramps that expose problems at low traffic before full deployment, and the ability to isolate or disable individual paths when a problem surfaces. OpenAI, 2026

The lesson for builders is specific. If your metrics combine media latency and logic latency into a single number, you will not know which one broke until both are broken.

What This Means for Your Voice Product: Three Architectural Decisions You Cannot Defer

GPT-Live now powers ChatGPT Voice, including agentic coordination and computer control. The architecture is becoming a broader platform for real-time interaction and will underpin the upcoming GPT-Live API. OpenAI, 2026

Whether you plan to build on that API or build your own voice stack, three architectural decisions carry forward from GPT-Live's engineering story:

Decision 1: Separate media flow from application logic. This is not an optimization. It is the foundation. Every downstream improvement in GPT-Live (the Go rewrite, WARP, Instant Connect, managed compaction) depends on this boundary existing. If your audio and your business logic share a thread pool, a slow tool call will freeze the conversation. Make the separation structural, not aspirational.

Decision 2: Build for continuous sessions, not request-response. Your capacity model, your state management, your shutdown handshake, and your observability all need to account for sessions that run for an hour and accumulate state the entire time. If you only test with short bursts, you will discover the memory leaks and reconnection bugs in production.

Decision 3: Instrument full-duplex metrics from day one. Barge-in stop latency, false barge-in rate, backchannel yield rate, and turn-onset latency must be measured from audio, not from logs. Roark, 2026 "First audio" and "first meaningful audio" are different measurements in a delegating architecture where the voice model may fill silence with an acknowledgment while waiting for a reasoning result. If you do not separate these metrics now, you will not know whether your system is fast or whether it is faking fast.

The Separation That Makes Everything Else Possible

GPT-Live's engineering story spans six months, multiple protocol innovations, a language rewrite, a new observability stack, and a new class of metrics. But every one of those changes traces back to a single early decision.

OpenAI separated media flow from application logic. OpenAI, 2026 That separation gave the system a clean boundary for customization, a guarantee that slow logic cannot stall audio, and an architecture that scales on two independent axes.

The turn detector was not beaten by a better model. It was made irrelevant by a better system. The question for your team is whether you will make that architectural separation before scale forces it on you, or after.
