Models Solve Math but Cannot Write Chapters Because the Reward Function Does Not Exist

RLVR forces us to see LLMs as agents in an environment, not just statistical models.

The Miracle That Didn't Generalize

The latest generation of frontier models can solve competition-level math problems. They write production-grade code that passes human-authored test suites. Ask those same models to produce a coherent chapter of non-fiction, and the result reads like committee notes assembled by people who never coordinated.

GPT 4.5 and Kimi K2 went from adequate to superhuman at mathematics and coding in a single model generation while making no visible progress on long-form writing quality Nathan Lambert, Interconnects AI, 2026. The gap between these two outcomes is not random. It traces to a specific training technique, one that has no equivalent for prose.

The technique is Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR), a training method where the model generates thousands of candidate solutions and checks each against a known correct answer. Math proofs resolve or they don't. Code passes a test suite or it fails. RLVR exploits this binary: generate, check, reinforce what worked. Think of a student who retakes the same exam ten thousand times with the answer key in hand until every problem becomes trivial.

Writing has no answer key. No checkable reward exists that can tell a training loop whether a chapter builds coherently across twenty pages, whether an argument's structure serves the reader, or whether a conceptual framing produces insight rather than noise. "Correct" in long-form prose is a judgment distributed across thousands of interdependent decisions. RLVR cannot operate in a domain where the reward function does not exist.

| Domain | Training Reward | Improvement (Latest Gen) | Status | |---|---|---|---| | Mathematics | Checkable (proof resolves) | Adequate to Superhuman | RLVR applied | | Code | Checkable (tests pass) | Adequate to Superhuman | RLVR applied | | Long-form prose | No checkable reward | Stagnant | No equivalent technique |

What Happens When Models Hit Long-Form

The stagnation is not abstract. A researcher who spent 2024 to 2026 writing a textbook on reinforcement learning from human feedback tested this gap at full scale. His fear before starting was that AI would age the book out before publication. That fear turned out to be wrong. By 2026, his confidence in the book's staying power was higher than when he started, because the models had failed to deliver on the writing hype.

The final book contained less than 1% of AI-generated technical explanation sentences, and those survived only when the author, as a domain expert, found the phrasing superior to his own Nathan Lambert, Interconnects AI, 2026.

The Failure Modes

The breakdowns follow a pattern:

- Models produce sentence-level prose that reads fine in isolation. At the chapter level, the output turns muddled in organization, sprinkled with confusing wording, and off in ways that are hard to pinpoint until you try to learn from the text. - Models attempt to be clever where clarity is the job. In the process, they introduce random conceptual errors that a non-expert reader would never catch.

These are not surface-quality issues. A spell-checker can catch typos. No current model can detect that its own chapter fails to build an argument.

The Compounding Entropy Problem

The root cause is not randomness. It is compounding organizational decay.

Models can check individual units of content: a sentence, an equation, a figure. They can produce any single component at a high level. What they cannot do is revise components and string them together into a coherent whole as they stack additions on top of each other. Each new paragraph introduces small misalignments. Over the length of a chapter, those misalignments compound into what feels like competent prose with no center.

The framing that makes this concrete: organizing knowledge is compression. A good textbook chapter takes a sprawling body of research and compresses it into a structure that creates insight. Current LLMs do the opposite in long-form non-fiction. They scatter rather than compress. They increase entropy. And that entropy cannot be stacked on top of itself without a human acting as a structural guide.

Independent research confirms this pattern from a different angle. A study examining LLM-generated long-form narratives found that models contradict their own established facts, character traits, and world rules as text grows longer. The distance between a stated fact and its contradiction matters: contradictions appearing soon after a fact point to local tracking failures, while contradictions appearing many paragraphs later point to long-range coherence breakdowns Li et al., ACL 2026 Findings. The model is not forgetting. It is failing to maintain an organizational structure that holds its own claims in place.

Why Inference-Time Scaling Didn't Fix It

The obvious question: if RLVR worked for math and code, why not build an equivalent for prose?

The answer has two parts.

The first is the reward function problem. Math and code have binary outcomes. Prose does not. There is no "test suite" for whether a chapter's argument holds together, whether its examples land, or whether its structure serves someone trying to learn the material. Building a reward function for long-form writing quality is an open research problem, not an engineering task.

The second is that inference-time scaling, the practice of giving models more computation before answering, has not been applied to writing the way it has been applied to reasoning tasks. When asked whether it maintained an iterative scratchpad before generating a poem, Claude Fable 5 reported that it had not. It made a minimal plan in its reasoning tokens and generated text from start to finish. No iteration. No revision loop. No advantage taken from extended computation.

This is how most people use models for writing, and mediocre results follow from mediocre process. The best results would require prompting models to plan at length, work through multiple drafts, and consult judge models before returning text. That workflow exists in research prototypes: outline-based planning, hierarchical decomposition, entity tracking, and dedicated coherence scoring are active areas of work Michael Brenndoerfer, 2026. None of these are standard inference behavior. The infrastructure for prose quality at scale does not exist in production.

The Larger Stakes: AI Scientists Building on Quicksand

The writing failure is not an isolated limitation. It is a legibility test for a larger claim.

Models that cannot organize and present established science in their own domain are being asked to solve open science problems on their own. This gets the order of operations wrong. Organizing known knowledge into a coherent argument is a prerequisite for generating new knowledge. If a model cannot compress existing research into a textbook chapter without a human steering the structure, the premise that it can tackle open-ended scientific questions without human guidance is not supported by the evidence.

The progress AI makes in science will continue. But that progress will look closer to solving specific, bounded problems and connecting distant findings across fields, not to the autonomous generation of novel insight. Novel insight requires the organizational capacity that long-form prose exposes as absent.

The timeline reinforces this: the best textbooks will be crafted by human hands for the next two to five years Nathan Lambert, Interconnects AI, 2026. That is longer than most AI optimism narratives assume, given how much knowledge these models contain and how fast they can produce it.

The Boundary Revealed: Where Models Deliver

The honest ceiling for AI-assisted writing effort savings is 10 to 20%, with no clear path to that becoming the majority anytime soon Nathan Lambert, Interconnects AI, 2026.

The pattern is visible once you map where models succeed and where they fail. Models excel in checkable domains and when given extensive context to make a small, bounded edit: finding a bug, solving a specific math problem, giving feedback on an existing draft. They fail at generating prose in an open-ended manner, because open-ended generation is the domain where the reward function problem hits hardest.

The specific wins are real. GPT models have been strong at finding typos for years. Passing a near-final 200 to 300 page manuscript PDF to GPT 5.5 Pro surfaced deep, surprising minor typos across the text. Claude models are more useful as editors: they carry more taste, understand the task's mental model better, and offer stronger suggestions for breaking through writer's block. Without AI agents, syncing between Markdown and LaTeX versions of a book would have taken five times as long, and the task already consumed tens of hours. Writing equations became trivial. Refactoring repositories and porting between languages worked well.

LLMs make good editors and bad writers. The line falls between verification (bounded, checkable, small-scope) and generation (open-ended, structural, long-range).

| Task | Checkable? | AI Capability | Effort Saved | Bottleneck | |---|---|---|---|---| | Typo-finding | Yes | Strong | High | None at current quality | | Editing and feedback | Partial | Strong (Claude leads) | Moderate | Taste and context limits | | Equation generation | Yes | Strong | High | None | | Structural syncing (Markdown/LaTeX) | Yes | Strong (5x faster) | High | Scope of repo | | Code refactoring and porting | Yes | Strong | High | Edge cases | | Long-form prose composition | No | Weak | 10 to 20% ceiling | No checkable reward |

Frontier models reflect this boundary at the top of the market. Claude Opus 4.6 leads prose benchmarks for coherence, maintaining voice and structure over thousands of tokens, but consistency bugs persist as the distance between related statements grows, at $25 per million output tokens LLM Stats, 2026. GPT-4o uses a 128k token context window and style-transfer prompting to produce high-coherence drafts, but both models carry higher output costs than midsize alternatives LLM Stats, 2026. Paying premium prices narrows the coherence gap at the margins. It does not close it.

Conclusion

The machinery is not broken. LLMs do what their training equipped them to do: excel in domains with checkable answers and struggle in domains without them.

The uncomfortable part is not the limitation. It is the distance between the limitation and the claims built on top of it. Inference-time scaling has not been applied to long-form writing, one of the great intellectual pursuits. Models will get bigger and hold more world knowledge, and small errors will improve. But the ability to organize that knowledge into compressed, insightful form is a different capability. More parameters do not produce organizational coherence.

Recent fine-tuning on curated high-quality books has improved voice stability on short excerpts, but current LLMs cannot write long-form narratives that balance coherence, quality, thematic consistency, and structural development across thousands of words arxiv 2601.18353v1, 2026. The researchers behind that work call the gap temporary. Temporary is a fair answer. Borrowed credibility is not.

The rule I follow: never use AI-generated outputs in non-fiction content. Using AI for the writing takes away the progression that builds understanding. Without that understanding, in the form of intuition, taste, and instinct, you cannot catch the model's flaws. Your pace of learning does not increase by delegating the thinking to an agent.

The writing failure is a canary. Until models prove they can organize and compress established knowledge without human shepherding, claims about autonomous scientific discovery are running ahead of the evidence that supports them.
