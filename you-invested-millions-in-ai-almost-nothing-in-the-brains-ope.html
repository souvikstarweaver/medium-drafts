You Invested Millions in AI. Almost Nothing in the Brains Operating It.

The 79% of companies deploying AI but seeing no EBIT lift are spending cognitive capital they never budgeted for.

Most organizations have placed a large bet on AI. The bet is not paying off, and the diagnosis that most leaders are landing on is wrong. They are blaming the technology: the model is not good enough, the data pipeline needs work, the vendor overpromised. The constraint is not the technology. The constraint is the cognitive capacity of the people using it.

I have watched this pattern repeat across three organizations now. Teams get access to powerful AI tools. Productivity ticks up for a quarter. Then the gains flatten, or worse, the quality of decisions starts degrading. The teams did not get dumber. The organization asked their brains to do new, harder work without changing a single thing about how it supported those brains.

This is the argument: the gap between AI adoption and AI results is a brain capital problem, not a technology problem. And there are five specific, operational principles that close the gap.

The AI Adoption Paradox: Why 79% Isn't Winning

The numbers tell a clean story. Seventy-nine percent of organizations have adopted generative AI, but only 39 percent of respondents attribute any level of EBIT impact to AI, and fewer than one-third report scaling AI beyond pilots (McKinsey/RamaOnHealthcare, 2026). Fewer than 25% of organizations achieve sustained performance improvement from their AI investments (McKinsey State of Organizations 2026).

Read those numbers together: the vast majority adopted. A minority saw returns. An even smaller minority sustained them.

The standard explanation is that these organizations need better models, better integration, or better data. The McKinsey framing says something different: "The constraint is not the technology" (McKinsey/RamaOnHealthcare, 2026).

| Metric | Number | What It Tells You | |---|---|---| | Organizations that adopted gen AI | 79% | Adoption is not the problem | | Organizations reporting any EBIT impact from AI | 39% | Most investments are not producing returns | | Organizations achieving sustained performance improvement | < 25% | Even initial gains tend to fade | | Organizations investing in people AND performance: financial outperformance multiplier | 4.3x | The returns come when brain capital is part of the investment (AI to ROI Substack, 2026) |

If it is not the technology, what is it? The answer lives in the human brain operating it.

The Brain Is the Real Bottleneck

Brain capital is a term that sounds like HR jargon. It is not. It is the sum of two things: brain health (optimal brain functioning, plus the prevention and treatment of mental, neurological, and substance use disorders) and brain skills (cognitive, interpersonal, self-leadership, and technological literacy abilities) (Coe, Brassey, Enomoto, Pérez / McKinsey Health Institute and World Economic Forum, 2026).

Think of brain capital like compute capacity in a cloud infrastructure. You can write the most elegant code in the world, but if the server it runs on is throttled, overheated, or running on degraded memory, your application underperforms. Brain capital is the human compute layer. AI is the application running on top of it.

Harris Eyre, a leading voice in brain economics, frames it plainly: "Brain capital is the new paradigm here. It's human capital 2.0, human capital in the age of neuroscience" (Health Policy Watch, 2026).

The critical reframe is this: AI's value depends on the quality of human judgment applied on top of it. Brain capital is the precondition for the AI agenda, not a wellbeing initiative developing beside it (Safetysure, citing Coe et al. MHI/WEF, 2026).

When I hear leaders talk about AI readiness, they mean data readiness, infrastructure readiness, and workflow readiness. None of them mean brain readiness. That is the gap.

Five Principles for Brain-Powered Organizations

McKinsey's research identifies five operational principles that address this gap (McKinsey and Company, 2026). These are not wellness tips. They are organizational design decisions. Each principle maps to a specific lever in the system that connects human cognition to AI output.

Principle 1: Calibrate Cognitive Load

This is the demand side. Match the intensity of work tasks to employees' cognitive capacity. As AI changes the nature of work, balance complex tasks with time for learning, recovery, and lower-intensity work (McKinsey and Company, 2026).

The key phrase is "as AI changes the nature of work." AI does not eliminate cognitive load. AI changes what the brain is asked to do (McKinsey and Company YouTube, July 9, 2026). It strips away the rote tasks and concentrates the remaining work into the hardest parts: judgment calls, exception handling, ambiguity resolution. That is a net increase in cognitive intensity per hour, not a decrease.

A PM reviewing ten AI-generated feature specs in a morning is doing harder cognitive work than writing two specs from scratch. The throughput looks higher. The cognitive cost is higher too. Calibrating load means treating that as a design problem, not an individual resilience problem.

Principle 2: Protect Cognitive Capacity

This is the supply side. Safeguard and restore the brain's resources. The foundation: sleep, recovery, and psychological detachment from work (McKinsey and Company, 2026).

An organization layering AI-driven intensity onto an already depleted workforce is spending brain capital it has not budgeted for (Safetysure, citing Brassey et al. McKinsey, 2026). That sentence is the entire argument of this article compressed into one line. Cognitive capacity depletes with sustained effort. It is not infinite. Organizations that treat it as infinite are running a deficit.

Principles 3, 4, and 5: Brain-Positive Environments, Enable Focus, Build the Adaptive Brain

Principle 3: Create and sustain workplace conditions that support brain capital. Principle 4: Create conditions for good judgment by protecting uninterrupted time for demanding decision-making. Principle 5: Upgrade how people think, learn, and adapt by embedding continuous skill development and flexibility for AI-driven change (McKinsey and Company, 2026).

Principle 4 deserves special attention. Gloria Mark's research on attention costs shows that every context switch carries a cognitive tax that accumulates throughout the day (Mark 2023, cited in Safetysure, 2026). AI tools increase context switches: reviewing AI output, correcting AI output, switching between tools, re-orienting after each review. Protecting uninterrupted time for judgment-intensive work is not a perk. It is an input to AI effectiveness.

Principle 5 confronts a hard number: 59% of employees are expected by their employers to need additional training by 2030 to meet evolving skill demands (WEF 2025, cited in Coe et al. 2026, via Safetysure). Two-thirds of the skills organizations need within five years will be different from the skills in demand today (McKinsey State of Organizations 2026). Building an adaptive brain is not about training people on the current tool. It is about building the cognitive flexibility to learn the next one.

When AI Offloading Becomes a Liability

The five principles are not theoretical. The evidence for what happens when organizations ignore them is accumulating fast.

In a 2026 study, 58 percent of participants agreed that AI "did most of the thinking," with the strongest effect on planning and sequencing work. Those participants reported reduced confidence in their own independent reasoning and a diminished sense of ownership over their output (Baldeo 2026 / APA Technology, Mind, and Behavior).

An MIT Media Lab preprint found that unassisted essay writers showed the strongest neural connectivity patterns, while AI-assisted writers showed the weakest. The AI-assisted participants also struggled to quote from essays they had produced minutes earlier (Kosmyna et al. / MIT Media Lab, 2025, cited in Safetysure, 2026). The tool produced the output, but the brain did not retain the knowledge.

This is not a new problem wearing a new coat. Lisanne Bainbridge documented it in 1983: automate the routine, and you leave a de-skilled human responsible for handling the abnormal situations automation cannot, at the moment their skills are least practiced. This pattern was documented in cockpits and control rooms. It now applies to general knowledge work (Safetysure, citing Bainbridge 1983, 2026).

In a BCG-Harvard study, consultants using AI were 19 percentage points less likely to produce the right answer on tasks that fell outside AI's competency zone (Dell'Acqua et al. / BCG-Harvard, via oneusefulthing.org). The tool made them faster on the tasks it could handle, and worse on the tasks that required independent judgment.

Active AI Use Builds Brain Capital. Passive Use Erodes It.

The same body of research reveals that the mode of engagement determines the outcome. Three distinct collaboration patterns have emerged (Resultsense, 2026):

| Mode | Behavior | Share | Cognitive Outcome | |---|---|---|---| | Cyborg | Continuous iterative dialogue with AI | 60% | Developed new AI-related expertise | | Centaur | Selective use with firm human control | 14% | Highest accuracy, deepened domain expertise | | Self-Automator | Passive delegation to AI | 27% | Became dependent, skills eroded |

The distinction between these modes is an organizational design choice, not a personality trait. Participants who modified or challenged AI outputs reported greater confidence and a stronger sense of authorship (Baldeo 2026 / APA, cited in Safetysure, 2026). Uninterrupted time for demanding cognitive work is a prerequisite for the kind of active engagement that builds expertise (Mark 2023, cited in Safetysure, 2026).

This is where Principles 4 and 5 become operational. Protect the time for people to think hard about AI outputs, and embed the expectation that AI outputs get challenged, not accepted. Organizations that do this get Cyborgs and Centaurs. Organizations that do not get Self-Automators.

The Financial Case for Brain Capital

The business case is straightforward. Organizations that invest in both people and performance are 4.3 times more likely to sustain top-tier financial results than organizations that focus on performance alone (McKinsey State of Organizations 2026, cited in AI to ROI Substack, 2026).

The opportunity cost of ignoring brain capital is large. The McKinsey Global Institute estimates AI-powered agents and systems could create $2.9 trillion in annual US economic value by 2030 (McKinsey/RamaOnHealthcare, 2026).Scaling evidence-based brain health interventions could generate up to $6.2 trillion in cumulative GDP gains (WEF/Shyam Bishen, cited in eMHIC, 2026). On the other side of the ledger, $1 trillion in productivity is lost each year due to mental health challenges in the global workforce (Business Collaborative for Brain Health, 2026).

The positive case exists too. In the BCG-Harvard study, consultants using AI with maintained expertise completed 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, and produced 40% higher quality results (Dell'Acqua et al. / BCG-Harvard, via oneusefulthing.org). The gain was not from AI alone. The gain came from AI applied by humans whose cognitive capacity was intact and whose expertise was engaged.

The Decision in Front of You

Fifty-four percent of leaders have already chosen to upskill, reskill, or redeploy affected employees rather than eliminate roles (McKinsey State of Organizations 2026, cited in AI to ROI Substack, 2026). The instinct is right. The execution is incomplete. Upskilling without protecting cognitive capacity is like upgrading software on a server that is running out of memory.

Diverse perspectives are a practical enabler of better decision-making and AI outcomes (McKinsey State of Organizations 2026). The five brain capital principles preserve that diversity by protecting domain expertise, building adaptive capacity, and preventing the cognitive convergence that passive AI use creates.

You have invested millions in AI. The question McKinsey is posing is the right one: how much have you invested in the brains using it (McKinsey/RamaOnHealthcare, 2026)? Map the five principles against your current AI transformation plan. Every principle that is absent from the plan is a line item of cognitive capital you are spending without budgeting for it.
